Pseudo-Random Distribution: Why 25% Is Not Always 25%

What This Guide Covers

A hero's Critical Strike says 25%. That does not mean every attack has a 25% chance. The game hides a counter that starts the chance far lower and raises it with every attack that fails. This system is called Pseudo-Random Distribution (PRD), and once you know it, you can use it.

This guide explains how it works and what you can do with that knowledge.

It's built in two parts, plus a short closing:

  • Part I - The Mechanics. What PRD is, the formula behind it, the numbers for every common chance, what moves the counter, and why streaks look the way they do.
  • Part II - Practical Applications. Priming, how to read an enemy's proc state, procs with a cooldown, miss chances, and the mindset that goes with all of it.

Short on time? Jump to the Summary at the end for the key takeaways, and the Quick Reference for every formula and table in one place.

Part I - The Mechanics
1. What PRD Is

Start with what most people assume. In true random, every attempt has the same chance, and it has no memory of earlier attempts. A coin flip works this way. Ten tails in a row don't make heads any more likely on the eleventh.

PRD works differently. The chance starts below the listed number and rises with every failed attempt, then resets when the effect triggers. The listed percentage is the long-run average, not the chance on any single attack.

PRD applies to "X% chance when Y happens" effects. Random amounts (such as attack damage rolling between a minimum and maximum) and random picks do not use it.

What uses it. The set currently includes effects like these, among others:

  • Phantom Assassin - Coup de Grace
  • Juggernaut - Blade Dance
  • Chaos Knight - Chaos Strike
  • Faceless Void - Time Lock
  • Spirit Breaker - Greater Bash
  • Items - Skull Basher, Crystalys, Daedalus, Maelstrom, Mjollnir
2. The Formula

The rule behind PRD is short:

Chance on attempt N = C × N

N counts the attempts since the last proc. C is a constant set by the listed chance - it's the chance on your first attempt after a proc, and the next section lists it for each common chance. When C × N reaches 100%, the proc is guaranteed.

Example. For a listed 25% chance, C is 8.5%:

  • First attack after a proc: 8.5%
  • Second attack: 17%
  • Third attack: 25.5%
  • And so on, 8.5% higher with every attack, until it procs.

Notice what that means. The third attack already has more than the listed 25%, but the first two had much less. Averaged over many procs, it comes out to 25%.

PRD is the opposite: the first attempts are weak, the chance peaks around the third and fourth, and by the tenth attempt it has nearly no probability left. The proc is pulled toward the middle.

4. What Moves the Counter

The counter doesn't care about every attack equally. Four things decide when it moves.

  • Only attempts that could trigger the effect count. Attacking a building does not build up a critical strike, because critical strikes do not work on buildings.
  • A cooldown pauses the counter. While the effect is on cooldown, attempts neither build the counter nor reset it. Details in Section 8.
  • The buildup does not expire with time. In testing, I made 4 attacks without a critical strike, paused for 60 seconds, then attacked again. The critical strike came within the first two attacks in 5 out of 5 trials.
  • The buildup carries across targets for effects that belong to the attacker. In testing, I built up on one target, switched to a second, and the proc came early on the second.

The last two are the foundation of everything in Part II.

5. How Often to Expect a Proc

Use two columns of the Section 3 table: average attempts per proc and most likely attempt.

A 25% chance means roughly one proc every 4 attacks, most often on the 3rd or 4th, and never later than the 12th. A 15% chance means roughly one proc every 6.7 attacks, most often on the 6th, and never later than the 32nd.

That "never later than" line is the part true random can't give you. Under true random, the longest wait has no limit at all.

6. Why Streaks Happen

PRD changes how streaks look, in both directions. Here are the odds for a 15% chance, counted right after a proc:

EventPRDTrue random
The next 2 attacks both proc (3 procs in a row)about 1 in 960about 1 in 44
No proc in the next 10 attacks13%20%
No proc in the next 20 attacksabout 1 in 9,000about 1 in 26

Short dry spells are normal. Long ones are nearly removed.

Back-to-back procs are rare, but a hero attacks so often that they still show up now and then, and they are memorable because they are rare.

Part II - Practical Applications
7. Priming

What priming is

Priming means attacking something unimportant until you have a run of non-procs, so your next attacks carry a raised chance. It works because the buildup does not expire and carries across targets (Section 4). Creeps, a neutral camp or a lane wave all work as the thing you build on.

Here is what that is worth. The tables show the chance of at least one proc in your first attacks of a fight:

Look at the "fresh" rows. Walking into a fight right after a proc is the worst state you can be in, well below true random. Walking in with 4 misses banked on a 25% chance gives you a 42.4% chance on the very first attack, and 88.5% within three.

The cost: if the proc fires on the creep, you are back to the worst state and need a new run of misses. That is free while farming.

Do not waste a primed attack

When you are primed, the first attack is the most valuable one. Spending it on a creep, an illusion or a ward in the middle of a fight throws the buildup away if it procs there. Pick the target that matters first.

Reading the enemy's proc state

PRD works the same against you. Right after an enemy procs on you, their next attacks are the safest you will face. With a 15% chance, they proc again within 3 attacks only 18% of the time (39% under true random). After a long run without a proc, the danger is at its peak.

Trading

Short trades get far less than the listed chance. A 25% proc in a 2-hit trade triggers 24% of the time (44% under true random). A hero with slow, heavy attacks facing a hero with fast proc attacks usually does better with short hit-and-run trades than with a long fight, if trading is all that is at stake.

8. Procs With a Cooldown

Some procs have an internal cooldown. During it, attacks do not build toward the next proc. I checked these in-game:

EffectCooldown
Spirit Breaker's Greater Bash1.2 seconds
Skull Basher2.3 seconds, stated on the item
Maelstrom and Mjollnir's Chain Lightning0.2 seconds, not shown anywhere on the item

The consequence is that attack speed gives less and less for these procs. At one attack every 0.4 seconds, 3 attacks fall inside a 1.2 second cooldown and count for nothing.

The 0.2 second cooldown on Chain Lightning only starts to matter near the attack speed cap. See the Base Attack Time guide for how attacks per second work at the cap.

9. Miss Chances

Ranged heroes attacking from low ground currently have a 25% chance to miss. All numbers in this section come from my own tests in Demo mode.

  • Same target. After an uphill miss, the next attack on that target hit 23 times out of 25 (92%). The base hit chance is 75%.
  • After switching to a different target. The next attack hit 42 times out of 49 (86%).

So a miss on a creep probably helps your next attack on a hero, but less than staying on the same target.

The application for ranged mid players: attack a creep on the high ground until you miss, then attack the hero.

Evasion. Evasion is listed as using PRD, but in testing with a 35% evasion item its streaks did not match the standard numbers, so this guide gives no streak table for evasion. The average was confirmed: 36% misses over more than 300 attacks.

10. The Mindset

A run of non-procs is the system working as designed, and the proc is mathematically on its way.

Non-procs on creeps right before a fight are good news, not bad luck. You are banking chance for the moment it matters.

Knowing this keeps you from tilting, or from leaving a fight that is about to turn.

Closing
11. A Short History of PRD

PRD comes from the Warcraft 3 engine, which the original DotA ran on.

That engine's constants were inaccurate for higher chances: a listed 50% delivered about 45.7% and a listed 80% about 66.7%. Up to 30% it was accurate. Dota 2 uses constants that average out to the listed chance.

12. Summary
  • The listed chance is an average. The chance on any single attack is different from it, and short sequences get less than the listed number.
  • The chance builds and resets. It starts low, rises with every failed attempt, is guaranteed at a fixed point, and resets on a proc. Chance on attempt N = C × N.
  • The buildup does not expire and carries across targets. Only attempts that could trigger the effect count.
  • Priming works. Run up misses on something unimportant before a fight, then spend the first primed attack on the target that matters.
  • Cooldown procs give less from attack speed. Attacks during the cooldown build nothing.
  • A run of non-procs is good news. The proc is on its way, so don't tilt and don't leave the fight.
