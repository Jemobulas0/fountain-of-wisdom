Pseudo-Random Distribution: Why 25% Is Not Always 25%

What This Guide Covers

A hero's Critical Strike says 25%. That does not mean every attack has a 25% chance. The game hides a counter that starts the chance far lower and raises it with every attack that fails. This system is called Pseudo-Random Distribution (PRD), and once you learn it, you can use it to gain the upper hand in your games in a way that almost nobody else ever does.

This guide explains how it works and what you can do with that knowledge.

It's built in two parts, plus a short closing:

  • Part I - The Mechanics. What PRD is, the formula behind it, the numbers for every common chance, what moves the counter, and why streaks look the way they do.
  • Part II - Practical Applications. Priming, how to read an enemy's proc state, procs with a cooldown, miss chances, and the mindset that goes with all of it.

Short on time? Jump to the Summary at the end for the key takeaways, and the Quick Reference for every formula and table in one place.

Part I - The Mechanics
1. What PRD Is

Start with what most people assume. In true random, every attempt has the same chance, and it has no memory of earlier attempts. A coin flip works this way. Ten tails in a row don't make heads any more likely on the eleventh.

PRD works differently. The chance starts below the listed number and rises with every failed attempt, then resets when the effect triggers. The listed percentage is the long-run average, not the chance on any single attack.

PRD applies to "X% chance when Y happens" effects. Random amounts (such as attack damage rolling between a minimum and maximum) and random picks do not use it. Not every chance-based effect uses it either. In my testing, Ogre Magi's Multicast is truly random.

What uses it. The set currently includes effects like these, among others:

  • Phantom Assassin - Coup de Grace
  • Juggernaut - Blade Dance
  • Chaos Knight - Chaos Strike
  • Faceless Void - Time Lock
  • Spirit Breaker - Greater Bash
  • Items - Skull Basher, Crystalys, Daedalus, Maelstrom, Mjollnir
2. The Formula

The rule behind PRD is short:

Chance on attempt N = C × N

N counts the attempts since the last proc. C is a constant set by the listed chance - it's the chance on your first attempt after a proc, and the next section lists it for each common chance. When C × N reaches 100%, the proc is guaranteed.

Example. For a listed 25% chance, C is 8.5%:

  • First attack after a proc: 8.5%
  • Second attack: 17%
  • Third attack: 25.5%
  • And so on, 8.5% higher with every attack, until it procs.

Notice what that means. The third attack already has more than the listed 25%, but the first two had much less. Averaged over many procs, it comes out to 25%.

4. What Moves the Counter

The counter doesn't care about every attack equally. Four things decide when it moves.

  • Only attempts that could trigger the effect count. Attacking a building does not build up a critical strike, because critical strikes do not work on buildings.
  • A cooldown pauses the counter. If the effect has a cooldown and is on said cooldown, attempts neither build the counter nor reset it. Details in Section 8.
  • The buildup does not expire with time. In testing, I made 4 attacks without a critical strike, paused for 60 seconds, then attacked again. The critical strike came within the first two attacks across all trials.
  • The buildup carries across targets for effects that belong to the attacker. In testing, I built up on one target, switched to a second, and the proc came early on the second across all trials.

The last two are the foundation of everything in Part II.

5. How Often to Expect a Proc

Use the table from Section 3.

A 25% chance means roughly one proc every 4 attacks, most often on the 3rd or 4th, and never later than the 12th. A 15% chance means roughly one proc every 6.7 attacks, most often on the 6th, and never later than the 32nd.

That "never later than" line is the part true random can't give you. Under true random, the longest wait has no limit at all.

6. Why Streaks Happen

The reason Pseudo-Random Distribution was invented was to avoid extreme cases occurring way too often. PRD changes how streaks look, in both directions. Here are the odds for a 15% chance, counted right after a proc:

EventPRDTrue random
3 procs in a rowabout 1 in 960about 1 in 44
No proc in the next 10 attacks13%20%
No proc in the next 20 attacksabout 1 in 9,000about 1 in 26

As seen in the table, only the middle example somewhat overlaps between PRD and True Random. Meanwhile the extreme cases of no procs occurring at all or way too many happening in a row are almost non-existent.

As to why we remember a lot of extreme cases? Let's say that you're playing Phantom Assassin. Stifling Dagger has double the chance to proc your ultimate, so through accidental or intentional priming you can land 3 out of 4 crits once every 4-8 games, which is often enough to feel like it's happening frequently, but rare enough to be an explosive, super memorable event. And it's extra memorable if it happens to you, rather than if you do it to somebody else, since the human brain is built to register the negative version of this event harder.

Part II - Practical Applications
7. Priming

What priming is

Priming means attacking something unimportant until you have a run of non-procs, so your next attacks carry a raised chance. It works because the buildup does not expire and carries across targets, as covered in Section 4. Lane creeps and neutral camps are your usual go-to options. Especially for the laning stage, if you don't want to influence the lane equilibrium for whatever reason, but you also can't really tank neutral creeps, a cool little trick is to pull them from their camp, and attack them as they walk back. This way you can Prime your next proc without practically losing any health.

Look at the 0 column. Walking into a fight right after a proc is the worst state you can be in, well below the listed chance. Walking in with 5 misses banked on a 17% chance gives you a 25% chance on your very first attack, and a 64% chance to proc at least once in your first three attacks. Fresh, those numbers are 4% and 23%.

The cost: if the proc fires on the creep, you are back to the worst state and need a new run of misses. That is free while farming.

Do not waste a primed attack

When you are primed, the first attack is the most valuable one. Spending it on a creep, an illusion or a ward in the middle of a fight throws the buildup away if it procs there. Prioritize the target that matters the most first.

Reading the enemy's proc state

PRD works the same against you. Right after an enemy procs on you, their next attacks are the safest you will face. With a 15% chance, they proc again within 3 attacks only 18% of the time (39% under true random). After a long run without a proc, the danger is at its peak. In an ideal scenario where both you and an opponent have procs, choosing to go in Primed yourself just after the enemy hero has procced gives you a huge advantage, depending on the circumstances of course.

Trading

Short trades get far less than the listed chance. A 25% proc in a 2-hit trade triggers 24% of the time (44% under true random). So if you're playing a hero with slow and heavy attacks like Chaos Knight, you want to utilize hit and run tactics around your procs. Vice versa, if you're a fast hitting hero like Juggernaut, you ideally want prolonged trades, as they are the ones in your favor. This again is an example of when both heroes have procs to play around. If you're the only one who has a proccable effect, then you can choose which tactic works better for you in any given situation.

8. Procs With a Cooldown

Some procs have an internal cooldown. During it, attacks do not build toward the next proc.

EffectCooldown
Spirit Breaker's Greater Bash1.2 seconds
Skull Basher2.3 seconds, stated on the item
Maelstrom and Mjollnir's Chain Lightning0.2 seconds, not shown anywhere on the item

The consequence is that attack speed gives less and less for these procs. At one attack every 0.4 seconds, 3 attacks fall inside a 1.2 second cooldown and count for nothing.

Proc Cooldown - the counter to truly broken builds. Certain proc effects have most likely been given cooldowns intentionally, because otherwise they would break the game. Skull Basher for example has one of the longest cooldowns in order to avoid perma-bash with high enough attack speed. But items like Maelstrom and Mjollnir have a very short cooldown to counter one specific mechanic abuse - multishot (and to a smaller extent Echo Sabre and Harpoon). Imagine the horror that Medusa and Muerta would inflict upon the world. At best, each one of their extra shots would progress the proc chance. At worst, multiple chain lightnings would proc at the exact same time. Or Medusa would simply bash the entire enemy team non-stop.

But for any other normal hero this is a non-issue for low cooldown proc effects. To have a problem with Maelstrom for example, you would need attack speed near the limit cap in order to start hitting faster than 0.2 seconds. Only Alchemist and Marci, with their ultimates, can attack faster than that. In my testing, heroes at exactly 0.20 seconds between attacks (Anti-Mage and Juggernaut at maximum attack speed) lost nothing, and attacking faster than 0.2 seconds never lowered overall DPS.

Attack speed begins to matter again the moment the cooldown wears off. So don't expect to perma-bash people by aiming for the maximum attack speed in the game.

9. Miss Chances

Ranged heroes attacking from low ground currently have a 25% miss chance. All numbers in this section come from my own tests in Demo mode.

After an uphill miss, my next attack hit 65 times out of 74 (88%). The usual hit chance is 75%.

So a miss on a creep helps your next attack on a hero.

The application for ranged mid players: attack a creep on the high ground until you miss, then attack the hero.

Of course make sure that you're not in range of the enemy hero while Priming - as always, keep the rule of Priming your hits only while being safely out of direct danger.

Evasion. Evasion is listed as using PRD, but in testing with a 35% evasion item its streaks did not match the standard numbers, so this guide gives no streak table for evasion. With a large enough sample, it should even out to exactly 35%.

10. The Mindset

Embrace Math! 💙

People fear the unknown. Be one of the few people who thrive in it while leaving all the others behind!

A streak of non-procs is the system working as intended. Your proc is mathematically on its way!

I love the lessons which DotA teaches, because they are often applicable 1 to 1 in real life as well:

The world is controlled chaos. The only thing that you're responsible for is to do your very best. All else is up to circumstances outside of your control. So Prime them hits and give yourself the best chances of success possible. From then on whatever happens - happens.

You will inevitably get bad streaks. Take comfort in the fact that you did your best! And when the good streaks happen - don't attribute those to dumb luck and thus deny yourself the pleasure of a job well done. We need those dopamine points baby - and now you can earn them!

Closing
11. A Short History of PRD

PRD comes from the Warcraft 3 engine, which the original DotA ran on.

I discovered Priming by myself all the way back in DotA 1, long before it became public knowledge. And I've been doing it ever since.

What I discovered only years later, after I was already playing DotA 2 for a while, was that the engine's constants in DotA 1 were inaccurate for higher chances: a listed 50% delivered about 45.7% and a listed 80% about 66.7%. It was only accurate up to 30%. DotA 2 uses better and more mathematically correct constants.

12. Summary
  • The listed chance is an average. The chance on any single attack is different from it, and your first hits after a proc get less than the listed number.
  • The chance builds and resets. It starts low, rises with every failed attempt, is guaranteed at a fixed point, and resets on a proc. Chance on attempt N = C × N.
  • The buildup does not expire and carries across targets. Only attempts that could trigger the effect count.
  • Proc effects with cooldowns. Attacks during the cooldown period do not build up towards your next proc.
  • Priming is your biggest practical application! Land the correct number of non-procs for your particular effect (as listed in my Tables) and then unload on your primary target.
  • Choose your strategy. Slow attacks and a low % proc chance work best with hit and run tactics. Vice versa, fast attacks and a high % proc chance excel at sustained aggression. Your opponents almost always fit into one of those two playstyles, regardless of whether they utilize proc effects and/or spells. So always be mindful of what your winning strategy is in any particular encounter.
